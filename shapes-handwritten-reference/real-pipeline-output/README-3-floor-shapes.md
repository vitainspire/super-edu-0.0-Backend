**Refresher** *(built from Topic 2's own Explore)*
1. Partners recall tracing round objects. Ask students to turn to a partner and describe what shape they got when tracing a round object at home. Wait 30 seconds for sharing.
2. Partners recall tracing straight-edged objects. Ask partners to share what shape they got when tracing a straight-edged object. Listen for correct naming of circles and rectangles.
3. Recall rangoli shapes. Ask students to raise hands and name any shapes they saw in rangoli patterns. Call on a few students, listening for circles, squares, triangles.

**Concept**
1. Straight edges fit without gaps. Place two matchboxes side-by-side on the blackboard, showing how their straight edges touch flush without any space. Draw the resulting shape. (Page 18)
2. Curved edges leave gaps. Place two bangles side-by-side on the blackboard. Show how their curved edges only touch at one point, leaving empty spaces or gaps. Draw the resulting shape. (Page 18)
3. Edges determine gap or no gap. Explain that whether shapes fit without gaps depends on their edges: straight edges touch flush, curved edges always leave space. (Page 18)

**Real Life**
1. Floor tiles use straight edges. Ask students if they have seen a tiled floor at home or in the village. Explain that floor tiles are usually square or rectangular (like a chouka) because their straight edges fit without gaps. (Page 17)
2. Matchboxes tile, bangles don't. Point to the picture of matchboxes arranged without gaps on page 18. Then point to the bangles arranged with gaps. Explain why one fits and the other doesn't. (Page 18)
3. Predicting gaps in floor patterns. Show the floor patterns on page 17. Ask students to identify the shapes. Explain that the shapes that fit perfectly have straight edges, while any curved shapes would leave gaps.

**Challenge — Gap or No Gap**
- *Play:* Pairs test matchboxes or bangles. Give each pair a choice: either two matchboxes or two bangles. Ask them to arrange their chosen objects on paper and observe if gaps remain. Allow 3 minutes.
- *Reflect:* Ask pairs to discuss for 30 seconds what they observed about the gaps (or lack thereof) with their chosen objects. Listen for mentions of straight vs. curved edges.
- *Act:* Identify floor shapes in book. Direct students to the floor patterns on page 17. Ask them to identify the shapes used in each floor and say whether those shapes would leave gaps. The matchbox shapes (rectangles) tile without gaps; the bangle shapes (circles) leave gaps.

**Level Set**
1. Recap gap rule, check misconception. Restate that straight edges fit without gaps, curved edges leave gaps. Ask partners if gaps depend on careful arrangement or the shape's edges.
2. Self-check with a tick list. Ask partners to list two things they learned today about shapes and gaps.
3. Dare: find tiling patterns outside. On the way home, look at a brick wall or a tiled path. Notice how the bricks or tiles fit together. Ask if they leave gaps and why. Nothing to bring back.

**Explore**
1. Tell students that on their way home, they should look at a tiled floor or a brick wall and notice whether the tiles or bricks fit together with no empty space between them.
2. Tell students to look for any floor or surface where objects are arranged, but there are small spaces between them. Ask them to think about why those spaces exist.
3. Tell students to think about why the tiles in a floor fit so well together without leaving gaps. Ask them to consider the shape of the tiles.

Materials used: matchbox, bangle, blackboard, textbook, paper.
Timings: Refresher 5, Concept 4, Real Life 5, Challenge 6, Level Set 4, Explore 6.

Mastery target: straight edges touch flush against a neighbour; curved edges only ever touch at one point, so they always leave a gap.
Named gap/misconception: whether a shape leaves gaps is random or just depends on how carefully you arrange it, not a property of the shape's edges.
