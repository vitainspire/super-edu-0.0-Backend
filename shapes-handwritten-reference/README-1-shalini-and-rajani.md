# Topic 1 — Shalini and Rajani like drawing pictures.
*(Views of an object: top, front, side — Grade 3 Maths, ts_scert_class3_maths_en, Chapter 1)*



**Concept**
1. Hold up your own textbook flat above your head, looking straight down at its cover. Ask: what shape do you see? (A flat rectangle.) That's the top view — one picture, nothing to compare yet. *(the floor — a single view, no comparison, no drawing needed)*
2. Now hold the SAME book at the edge, so the class sees it from the front. Ask: same shape, or different? (Very different — thin, not flat.) Draw both outlines on the blackboard side by side. Repeat quickly with a cup: its top is a plain circle, its side has a curved handle shape. A picture only shows what your eyes catch from ONE angle; look from a new angle and you see something new, even though the object itself never changed. *(the first real comparison — a small handheld object, no walking, no disruption)*
3. If a child says "that circle doesn't look like a cup," the teacher tips the cup back to its familiar side view right there, showing the exact same cup make that unfamiliar round shape a moment ago — proving a strange-looking picture can still be the honest, true view of something familiar.

**Real Life**
1. In the kitchen, a kunda (water pot) seen from straight above looks like a plain circle; seen from the side, it shows its rounded, curved body. Same pot, two honest views.
2. Ask the class: from which direction does a tawa (flat pan) look like a plain circle? Let one or two guess, then say plainly — straight from above; from the side, it's a thin flat line.
3. Point to a school bag hanging on the wall. From the front, it's a wide rectangle; turned to the side, it looks like a thin strip — same bag, two honest views, exactly like the chair.

**Challenge — Is This a Chair? (Identify and Draw Object Views)**
*Matches the book's own activity question: "Has Shalini drawn the picture of a chair? What do you think?"*
- *Play:* In pairs, first open the matchbox tray flat on the desk and decide together: does it look more like a rectangle or a square from straight above? Once you agree, look at the book's two chair pictures and decide together: is the top-view one really a chair, or not? (Floor: if the chair pictures are still hard, stay with the tray and try naming its shape from one more angle.)
- *Reflect:* Turn-and-tell your partner — which was easier to decide, the tray shape or the chair question, and why?
- *Act:* The teacher confirms: yes, it's the same chair, just seen from directly above — same idea as the cup in Concept. Then the teacher works through the FIRST house picture out loud — "this one has a peaked roof shape, which is what you'd see from the front, so I'll write front here" — before the class labels the other two pictures ("top," "side") the same way, on their own.

**Level Set**
1. Recap: one chair can make three honest but different pictures — top, front, side — because a picture only shows the angle you're looking from. Partners check each other: if someone says a top-view picture must be the "wrong" object, is that right? (No — it's still the same real thing.)
2. Ask a few children which view of an everyday object surprised them the most today — no right or wrong answer, just noticing.
3. On the way home, or once there, look at one thing from directly above and then from the side — a bucket, a pot, a stool — and notice how different the two pictures in your head are, even though it's the exact same thing.

**Explore**
1. Tell students that on their way home, or once there, they should look at one round vessel — a bucket, a pot, a drum — from straight above, then from the side, and notice that the top view looks like a plain circle while the side view shows its real height and curve.
2. Tell students to look at their own house's roof from inside, and notice that it looks completely different from how the house looks when they stand outside and see its front wall.
3. Tell students that tomorrow, they should be ready to say one object they looked at from two different angles, and how the two pictures were different — we'll compare what everyone noticed.

*Board sketch: a bucket or pot, drawn as a plain circle from above and a curved body from the side.*

Materials used: book, cup, water pot, tawa, school bag, textbook (chair pictures, house picture), matchbox tray.
Timings: Refresher 0 (first topic), Concept 6, Real Life 4, Challenge 8, Level Set 5, Explore 5.

Floor (weakest child): given the same simple object (a book), can say whether its top view and front view look the same or different, just by looking at the real book from both sides.
Named gap/misconception (textbook-sourced): a picture of an object that looks unfamiliar (like a chair seen from directly above) may make a child think it isn't a picture of that object at all — echoing Rajni's own line, "This is not a chair." Addressed twice, deliberately, not redundantly: Concept closes the general version of the gap with the cup (teacher-led, no book needed); Challenge resolves the textbook's own chair question directly, in the students' own hands — reinforcement from a second angle, not a repeat of the first.
