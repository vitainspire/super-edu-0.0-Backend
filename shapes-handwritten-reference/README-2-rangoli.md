**Refresher** *(built from Topic 1's own Explore ending)*
1. Yesterday you were told to notice an object from directly above and from the side. Ask two or three children to say what they noticed and which object they chose.
2. Turn and tell your partner what you noticed — did the top view and side view of your object look the same, or different, and why?
3. Just like a chair's shape depends on the angle, today we'll see how shapes can hide inside a busy picture, and we have to look carefully to find each one.

**Concept**
1. Draw one plain, isolated circle on the blackboard, away from anything else. Ask the class to name it — one shape, alone, easy to see. *(the easy entry)*
2. As the chapter's rangoli shows, what looks like one busy pattern is really several separate shapes — circles, triangles, squares — placed edge to edge or overlapping. Circle ONE shape at a time in chalk on a rangoli sketch, because circling separates what the eye first sees as one tangled picture.
3. If a child says the rangoli is "too mixed up" to name shapes in, the teacher covers all but one section with a hand or paper, showing just that one shape at a time until it's isolated and nameable — then removes the cover to show the same shape was there all along.

**Real Life**
1. In many villages and towns, families draw muggu (rangoli) patterns outside their front door, especially for festivals — the same circles, triangles and squares from today's lesson, arranged into a bigger pattern.
2. Ask the class: in a decorated temple gate or floor design they've seen, which single shape do they remember most? Let one or two guess, then say plainly that triangles and circles are common because their edges fit neatly.
3. Point out that a shopkeeper's gajulu (bangles) laid out on a cloth for sale make rows of circles — the same shape found again and again in a muggu.

**Challenge — Identify and Colour Rangoli Shapes**
- *Play:* In pairs, take the rangoli picture. Find and circle 3 triangles OR 3 circles in the pattern, whichever your pair finds first. (Floor: find just one of each shape first.)
- *Reflect:* Turn-and-tell — which shape was hardest to spot, and why?
- *Act:* Colour the rangoli as the page asks. The teacher states the answer for one tricky shape first before pairs finish the rest.

**Level Set**
1. Recap: a rangoli that looks like one busy picture is really several separate circles, triangles and squares. Partners check: if a child says "a rangoli is its own new shape, not made of others," is that right? (No — it's built from simple shapes.)
2. Ask which shape was the most fun to find today — no wrong answers.
3. On the way home, notice one rangoli, gate design, or tiled floor, and try to name two different shapes inside it.

**Explore**
1. Tell students that on their way home, or once there, they should look at any rangoli, gate design, or decorated wall near their house, and find and name at least two different shapes hiding inside it.
2. Tell students to look at a saree, bedsheet, or printed cloth at home, and notice which simple shape repeats the most.
3. Tell students that tomorrow, they should be ready to name the one shape they found most of — we'll compare what everyone noticed.

*Board sketch: a rangoli with one triangle circled separately from the rest of the pattern.*

Materials used: rangoli picture, chalk, bangles, printed cloth.
Timings: Refresher 3, Concept 6, Real Life 4, Challenge 8, Level Set 5, Explore 5.

Floor (weakest child): given just one clearly isolated shape (a plain circle alone on the board), can name it correctly, before being asked to find shapes hidden inside a busy pattern.
Named gap/misconception: children may treat overlapping/adjacent shapes inside a busy rangoli as one indistinguishable pattern rather than separate nameable shapes.
