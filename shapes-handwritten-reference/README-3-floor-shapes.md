**Refresher** *(built from Topic 2's own Explore ending)*
1. Yesterday you were told to notice a rangoli or pattern and find the shape that repeated most. Ask two or three children to name their shape.
2. Turn and tell your partner which shape you found most, and whether it matches theirs.
3. Some of those shapes — like squares — can sit right next to each other with no space left over. Circles can't. Today we find out why.

**Concept**
1. Give a pair of matchboxes to the front row. Push their straight edges together. Is there any gap? (No — the easy entry.)
2. As the chapter shows, tracing a matchbox and a bangle gives two different outlines. Push two matchboxes together — their straight edges touch flush along the whole line, because a straight edge lies completely flat against another straight edge. Push two bangles together — their curved edges touch at only one point, because a curve bends away almost everywhere else, leaving a gap on both sides.
3. **If a child says "squares just tile and circles just don't, that's the rule,"** the teacher pushes two matchboxes together and asks the child to point exactly where they touch (the whole edge), then pushes two bangles together and asks them to point where THEY touch (just one spot) — so the child sees the actual reason, not just the label.

**Real Life**
1. In most homes and courtyards, the nela (floor) is laid with square or rectangular tiles, edge to edge with no gaps, because their straight sides fit flush against the next tile's straight side.
2. Ask the class: could you tile a floor using only gajulu (bangles), with no gaps? Let one or two guess, then say plainly — no, because a bangle's curved edge only ever touches its neighbour at one point.
3. Point out that round rotis placed side by side on a plate always leave curved gaps between them, the same way gajulu do, because they share the same curved-edge property.

**Challenge — Arrange Matchboxes and Bangles Without Gaps**
- *Play:* In pairs, take 4 matchboxes and 4 bangles. Try to arrange each set on paper with no gaps between them. (Floor: try with 2 of each first.)
- *Reflect:* Turn-and-tell — which set was harder to arrange without gaps, and why?
- *Act:* Look at the two floor pictures on your page. The teacher works through the FIRST one out loud — "these tiles are squares, so their straight edges touch flush — I'll write 'straight edges, no gap' here" — then the class labels the second picture the same way, on their own.

**Level Set**
1. Recap: straight-edged shapes tile a floor with no gaps because their whole edge touches the next one; curved shapes always leave a gap because they only touch at one point. Partners check: "can a triangle tile a floor with no gaps?" (Yes — it also has straight edges.)
2. Ask which arrangement — matchboxes or bangles — surprised them the most today, and why.
3. On the way home, find one straight-edged thing and one curved thing, and notice whether each sits flush against its neighbour or leaves a gap.

**Explore**
1. Tell students that on their way home, they should look at the floor or wall tiles at home or in a shop, and notice whether the tiles are straight-edged or curved, and whether there are gaps.
2. Tell students to find one straight-edged object and one curved object at home, place each next to a matching twin, and notice which one leaves a gap.
3. Tell students that tomorrow, they should be ready to say which thing they checked left a gap and which didn't — we'll compare what everyone found.
