**Concept**
1. Hold up one small hand tool — a trowel. Ask: what is this for? (Digging small holes.) One tool, one job — nothing to compare yet. *(the floor — a single tool, no comparison needed)*
2. Now hold up a second small tool next to it — a sickle. Ask: could you dig a hole with this flat blade? (No — wrong shape.) Could you cut a stalk with the trowel's point? (No — also wrong.) As the chapter shows, farming has many stages — preparing land, sowing, harvesting — and *each stage needs its own tool*, the same way a kitchen needs a different tool for cutting than for stirring (Page 27). *(the first real comparison — two small handheld tools, no walking, no disruption)*
3. If a child says "farmers could just use any tool for any job, or their hands," the teacher tries digging with the sickle's flat edge right there in front of the class — showing it barely scratches the soil — then switches to the trowel to show it works easily. The wrong tool doesn't fail because it's bad, it fails because it's the wrong shape for that one job.

**Real Life**
1. A *raitu* (farmer) here in Telangana grows the rice or cotton on the land, but doesn't stop there — food still has to reach the village market and the city. (Page 26)
2. Point to the picture of a bullock cart loaded with sacks (Page 26). Ask the class: how do you think this food travels from a field to a shop in the city? Let one or two guess, then say plainly — by cart, then truck, then finally the shop shelf, with people working at every step.
3. Point out that behind every meal — rice, vegetables, even the milk in their tea — there's a *raitu* who grew it, and several more people who moved it, before it ever reached their plate.

**Challenge — "Visit the Farmer" (a real book task)**
*Matches the book's own activity on page 26: visit a nearby farmer and find out details about their farming.*
- *Play:* In pairs, choose ONE half of the real interview to prepare first: EITHER the tool questions (what equipment/tools do they use? where do the seeds come from?) OR the harvest questions (what do they do with their yield? how many acres do they farm?). Whichever half your pair picks is the one you prepare fully. (Floor: if choosing feels hard, start with just one single question from either half and build from there.)
- *Reflect:* Turn-and-tell — which question do you think will be hardest to get a clear answer to, and why?
- *Act:* The teacher works through how to ask the FIRST tool question out loud — "I'd ask 'what tools do you use for planting?' and listen for a specific tool name, not just 'farming tools'" — then the class prepares to ask their own chosen half's remaining questions the same way, on their own.

**Level Set**
1. Recap: farming has many stages, and each stage needs its own tool, because the right tool fits the job. Partners check each other: if someone says "one good tool should work for every farming job," is that right? (No — a tool built for one job is usually wrong for another.)
2. Ask a few children which farming tool they'd most like to actually try using — no right or wrong answer, just curiosity.
3. On the way home, or once there, notice one real tool being used for one real job — in a kitchen, a field, or a workshop — and think about whether a different-shaped tool could do that same job as well.

**Explore**
1. Tell students that on their way home, or once there, they should notice one farm tool in their village — old or new — and be ready to say what job it's built for.
2. Tell students to ask a *raitu* or an elder in their family which crop is grown nearest their home, and to remember the name.
3. Tell students that tomorrow, they should be ready to say the one tool they noticed and the one job it does — we'll compare what everyone found.
