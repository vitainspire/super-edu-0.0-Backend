# Differentiation

A single nominal grade is rarely a single level (`core-pedagogy`). The room
contains children two years behind the syllabus and children who will finish
your activity in a third of the time, and there is **one teacher**. So the unit
of differentiation is not the child and not the group. It is **the task**: one
task, entered at different depths, with a guaranteed bottom and an open top.

## Three standards, not one

The pipeline already carries two. Name the third:

    floor          what the child furthest behind leaves able to do. Every
                   child in the room reaches this or the period failed.
    gained         what THIS period lands for a child at grade level.
    masteryTarget  what the idea requires of a child deciding an unseen case.
                   Chapter-scale.

The floor is **not a lowered standard** and it is not the target. The target
does not move; the floor is the guarantee underneath it - the part of the
target that is reached in the room, by everybody, before the bell. A period
with no floor is a period that quietly sorts the class into those who kept up
and those who did not.

## Writing a floor

One sentence, the child's own voice, and **physically demonstrable in under
thirty seconds** by a child who cannot read the page:

    no    understands that a 2D view represents a 3D object
    yes   "I can stand over my school bag and point to what I would see"

    no    can identify place value in three-digit numbers
    yes   "I can put 24 bottle caps into two piles of ten and four left over"

    no    recognises overlapping shapes in a composite figure
    yes   "I can trace one triangle in this figure with my finger"

Three tests it must pass:

1. **Doable without reading.** The child furthest behind may not decode the
   page. If the floor requires reading, it is not a floor.
2. **Observable from across the room.** A teacher with forty children checks
   floors by looking, not by marking.
3. **On the same road as the target.** The floor is the first step of the real
   idea, not an easier neighbouring idea. Tracing one triangle leads to
   counting all of them. Colouring the figure in does not.

Where the trajectory (`experience-design`) is written, the floor sits on one of
its early-to-middle steps - usually the step just before the conceptual jump.
Mark which.

**Prefer a floor that is also the application.** G1 asks for the concept *and*
what it is for, and the child furthest behind is the one most likely to get the
concept without the use. A floor phrased as a use serves both at once:

    fine     "I can point to the flat side of my tiffin box"
    better   "I can tell if my book will lie flat in my bag"

## Checking a floor with forty children

Naming a floor creates an obligation to know it was reached, and per-child
assessment is not available. So the check is **one 20-second whole-class
action**, run once, usually late in Challenge:

    everybody hold up / point at / trace it now - teacher scans the room
    thumbs on the chest: could you do it? (`engagement`)
    teacher walks one row while the class works, not all six

What the teacher does with the count matters more than the count: if a large
part of the room did not reach it, the next period's Refresher repairs it
rather than the chapter moving on. Say that in the plan - a floor check with no
consequence written next to it changes nothing.

## The two-grade room

Where one teacher has two syllabi in the room (`core-pedagogy`), the floor and
the ceiling do more work than usual: **one object, two grades, different
questions.** The younger grade's required demand is often the older grade's
floor, so the same matchbox on the same desk carries "point to the top" and
"predict which flat shape folds into it" in the same three minutes. Where a
topic allows that, say so explicitly - it is the difference between a period
that runs in a combined room and one that does not.

## Step-scoring: how the middle holds

Most tasks in primary maths and EVS have a "find/count/name all the X" shape,
and a below-grade child produces **nothing** on them: they start at the whole
figure, cannot see it, and stop. The fix is not fewer X.

    WRONG   count fewer shapes because the class is below grade level
            (a smaller target - forbidden)
    RIGHT   find and mark the smallest single shapes first, then the ones made
            of two, then the whole figure
            (same total, entered in scoreable steps)

Written this way the child who never reaches the whole figure still has
completed, visible, correct work - and the child who reaches it has done the
same task. Anything with a total, a set or a list can be step-scored this way.
Where it cannot, say so.

## Leaving the top open

The fast finisher is the child the pipeline teaches least. What they need is
**demand raised on the same content**, and the invitation costs nothing:

    same materials       no second sheet, no new object
    open-ended           more than one right answer, so it cannot be finished
    no marking           the answer is shown to a neighbour or the row, not
                         collected
    same concept         it goes deeper into this idea, not sideways into the
                         next chapter

Examples of the move from closed to open on identical materials:

    closed  make a square with matchsticks
    open    make a shape with exactly ten matchsticks that is not a square or
            a rectangle, then say why it still has the same number of sides
            as your neighbour's different shape

    closed  count the triangles in the figure
    open    add one line to the figure so it has exactly two more triangles

    closed  write 342 in the place value chart
    open    make a three-digit number with these caps where changing one cap
            changes the number by a hundred, and show me which cap

See `stretch-design` for the full set of moves and the demand ceiling question
(short answer: the *required* demand stays inside the grade band; an *invited*
stretch may sit above it, because failing an invitation costs the child
nothing).

## The three-question pattern

The cheapest way to hold all three depths in one breath. Same object, three
questions, asked in this order so that each child hears one they can answer:

    1. point / name    "everyone show me the flat side"        (floor)
    2. compare / do    "which of these two would you see from
                        the top?"                              (gained)
    3. invent / break  "find something in the room where the
                        top and the side look the same"        (stretch)

Every child answers 1. Nobody is excluded from 2. Nobody is idle after 2.

## Grouping, when the activity needs groups

Mixed ability by default: one capable child, two average, two struggling in a
group of five (`activity-design`). Same-ability grouping tells thirty children
where they rank and leaves the bottom group with nobody to learn from.

A capable child explaining to their row is legitimate and free - but it is
**not** their stretch. They get the open prompt too. Explaining is a job;
stretch is teaching.

## Read the plan twice before submitting

Once as the child two years behind: what am I doing at minute 4, 12, 20? At
which minute do I stop understanding? Is there a floor on the other side of
that minute?

Once as the child who is done at minute 12: what am I doing for the remaining
ten minutes? "Helping others" for ten minutes is not an answer.

If either read produces a stretch of dead minutes, the plan is not finished.
