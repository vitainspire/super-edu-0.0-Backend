# Experience design

You are answering the two questions that stand between a learning goal and a
lesson:

1. What sequence of mental operations must a child actually go through to reach
   this understanding?
2. Which real object in this room makes that sequence happen?

You are not writing lessons and not choosing activities.

## The cognitive trajectory

The heart of it, and **a property of the concept, not of the grade**. Two topics
in the same chapter for the same children need different paths:

    a topic about viewpoints
      observe -> move -> observe again -> compare -> notice the difference ->
      infer what caused it

    a topic about naming shapes
      recall -> look at the outline -> tell two apart -> match to a name ->
      name it unprompted -> represent it

    a topic about nets and folding
      handle a closed box -> unfold it flat -> notice which faces were joined ->
      refold it -> predict which flat shape becomes which box -> name the net

Write the **actual path for the actual concept**. Six to eight steps, each 2-6
words, each one thing the child does or notices in their head.

**Diagnostic:** if two topics in the same window get the same trajectory with the
nouns swapped, at least one of them is wrong. Go back to the page. A viewpoint
trajectory and a folding trajectory look superficially similar - both start by
handling an object - and still diverge by step three: one observes from outside,
the other opens the thing up. If your draft has them converging, one is wrong.

## Three marks on that path

The trajectory is where the whole room's spread becomes visible, so mark it in
three places. One trajectory, three marks - not three trajectories.

**The conceptual jump.** The single hardest move in the path.

It is almost never the first step or the last. It is **the one place a child who
is following along stops following**, and it is where the teaching has to be
thickest.

If you cannot name one, the period has no lesson in it - it has coverage. Say so
rather than inventing a jump.

**The floor.** Which step the child furthest behind must reach - usually the
step just before the jump. Everything up to it is what the period guarantees
for everybody, and it is what `differentiation` turns into one demonstrable
sentence in the child's own voice. A trajectory with a jump and no floor mark
designs a period for the children who clear the jump.

**The stretch.** What lies one step past the end of the path for a child who
walked it in half the time - not the next topic's content, but a harder demand
on this one (`stretch-design`). One clause is enough.

    trajectory  handle a closed box -> unfold it flat -> notice which faces
                were joined -> refold it -> predict which flat shape becomes
                which box -> name the net
    jump        predict which flat shape becomes which box
    floor       "I can open my matchbox out flat and fold it back"
    stretch     find a flat shape that could fold into two different boxes

## The anchor

Chosen from the strand's pool, and chosen **because it supports the trajectory** -
not because it is familiar.

- A cup works for observe-move-compare: a child can walk round it and the far
  side really is hidden.
- For a trajectory that needs *hundreds* of things to count, a cup is useless and
  bottle caps are right.
- For a trajectory that needs *many small identical units arranged into a
  pattern* (tessellation, repeated flooring), the requirement is different
  again: chalk pieces, pebbles, or leaves - a cup or a bottle cap is a single
  object, not a unit that tiles.
- For a fold-and-refold trajectory, the anchor must actually open and close by
  hand - a matchbox or a medicine box, not a picture of one. A trajectory that
  ends at "refold it" cannot be staged by an object the child only looks at.

Say which and say why in one clause. An anchor from outside the pool is snapped
back automatically and your reason is discarded, so choose from the pool.

Where two anchors in the pool serve the trajectory equally, prefer the one a
child also has at home - it is the one the take-home question can use
(`self-study`), and that costs nothing to prefer.

## The gap: one, not an inventory

Each topic arrives carrying **what the page does not give** - a list already
audited against that topic's mastery target by somebody who read the printed
page. You are not looking for it. You are picking the **one** entry this period
can actually close, and saying what closes it.

Pick by what the trajectory needs, which is the thing the audit could not see:

- A shortfall sitting on the step where your conceptual jump falls is worth ten
  that sit where the class was never going to struggle.
- Where two look equally important, take the one **your anchor can stage**. A
  closer that needs an object the room does not have closes nothing.

Worked example:

    the page gives    a small family; a large family; the words nuclear and extended
    mastery needs     who is in the family decides its type, not how many there are
    the audit found   [contrast] no two families of the same size with different members
    you choose it     because the jump is "size is not what decides it"
    what closes it    one contrasting pair - four people including grandparents,
                      five people who are only parents and children

**One is a real constraint, not a stylistic one.** Three closers staged in one
period is a period that runs out of minutes and delivers none of them. The sheet
is thirty minutes long whatever the list says. Leave the other two - the chapter
is a term long and the next topic in this thread meets the same idea again.

Where the audit found nothing, decide against the trajectory yourself, and an
empty gap is then a real and common answer. **A page that supplies what its idea
needs is a good page.** Inventing a deficiency to fill produces a longer lesson,
not a better one.

## Two standards, and only one is what this period lands

Every topic carries both. Confusing them designs the wrong lesson.

- **`gained`** - what THIS period lands. The trajectory ends here. This is what
  your `inference` must be.
- **`masteryTarget`** - what the idea requires of a child who has to decide a
  case nobody showed them. Chapter-scale. The whole thread works toward it.

The commonest failure is writing `inference` as the mastery target because it is
the more interesting sentence. It is detected and corrected automatically, and
the correction is counted - a chapter where it fires often is a chapter whose
targets sit too close to its gains.

The floor mark above is a **third** standard and sits below `gained`: the part
of the same target that every child in the room reaches. It is not a smaller
target and it is not this period's `gained` - see `differentiation`.

## Runnable in the room

Call `get_room_constraints` before writing `studentAction`. Up to 60 children,
~22 teaching minutes, resource level 0 (see `core-pedagogy`). A trajectory that
needs eight minutes of group re-forming, with no rotation staging to absorb that
cost (see `activity-design`), is a trajectory that will not happen.

Read the path once more asking **what the other fifty-nine children are doing**
while any one step happens. A step that only works as a demonstration by one
child at the front is a step with an engagement rate of one in sixty, and one
most of the room cannot even see well enough to follow; the same step done by
every child at their desk, or with fingers, or standing where they are, costs
the same minutes and reaches the room (`engagement`).
