# Early mathematics

**Never explain a rule and then test it.** Number sense emerges by handling
concrete things - stones, bottle caps, fingers, the children's own bodies -
*before* symbols. A rule stated first and practised after produces a child who
can complete the exercise and cannot answer the same question phrased differently.

## What a maths trajectory looks like

Maths trajectories tend to run:

    handle -> arrange -> notice a pattern -> predict -> check -> name it

The conceptual jump is almost always at **predict**: the first moment the child
commits to an answer before counting. Everything before it is arrangement;
everything after is notation.

The **floor** for most maths periods sits at *arrange*: a child who cannot yet
predict can still make the piles, stand in the line, or hold the digit - and
that is real, visible, correct work on the same road (`differentiation`).

## Counting needs hundreds of things

The single commonest anchor mistake in primary maths. A trajectory about place
value or grouping needs objects there are *hundreds* of - bottle caps, seeds,
tamarind stones, sticks, beads. A water bottle is a fine anchor for shape and
useless for counting.

## Misconceptions this subject reliably produces

Look for these before inventing any:

- **The digit is just a digit.** "The 2 in 25 is a two." Place value has not
  landed; the child reads a numeral as two separate numbers.
- **Bigger number, bigger everything.** Length, weight and quantity collapse into
  one word.
- **Operations are symmetric.** "3 + 5 is 8, so 3 - 5 is 2 the other way."
- **The remainder is a mistake.** Every worked example on the page divides
  evenly, so a remainder reads as the child's error.
- **Zero is nothing, so it does nothing.** In 205, the zero is skipped.
- **The equals sign means 'the answer comes next'** rather than 'the same as'.
  This one survives to secondary school.

## Concrete staging that works at forty children

- **Human number line** - a line on the floor, children stand where their card
  belongs. Whole class, no materials.
- **Human number machine** - each child holds a digit; the group arranges itself
  into a called number. Swap two children, ask what changed. This one teaches
  place value better than any chart.
- **Skip-counting jump path** - numbers chalked on the floor, children hop by
  2s, 5s, 10s.
- **Human bar graph** - children stand in columns by their answer; the class
  becomes the graph.
- **Four corners** - agree / disagree / not sure corners for a statement. This is
  the cheapest Level Set staging there is.

All of these need chalk and a floor.

**Everybody-at-once devices, for the minutes between activities:** show the
answer on your fingers; thumbs on the chest for yes/no/not sure; everybody say
the number together; tell your neighbour which is bigger. Free, instant, and
they keep the participation rate near one (`engagement`).

## Where maths is used, in this room

The application is the half of the concept children usually never meet. Keep it
to things present in front of them (`real-world-connection`):

- sharing - "thirteen chalk pieces between four rows: how many each, and what
  is left over?" (this is division *and* the remainder misconception)
- fitting - "will this book lie flat in your bag or must it go sideways?"
- measuring with the body - hands, feet, steps across the room
- comparing - "who has more, and how do you know without counting one by one?"
- money as it is actually met - "these two things cost 6 and 7 rupees, is 15
  enough?"

## Take-home questions that cost nothing

One question, no adult, no purchase, self-checkable (`self-study`):

- count the steps across your room; ask someone smaller to do it too - who took
  more, and why, when the room did not change?
- find three round things at home and one that is nearly round
- how many cups of water fill the bucket? Guess first, then check
- find a number written somewhere at home and tell us tomorrow how many tens
  are in it
- put ten small things in a pile and ten in another - now how many without
  counting again?

## Stretch prompts, same materials

One sentence, more than one right answer, no marking (`stretch-design`):

- "make a number with these caps where changing one cap changes it by a
  hundred - show me which cap"
- "what is the smallest number of matchsticks that still makes three
  triangles?"
- "find two different ways to make 24 with tens and ones"
- "make one where that trick does not work"
- "does that work for every number, or only this one? How would you find out?"

## Notation last, and named

When the symbol finally arrives, it names something the child has already done.
Say which: "this mark means the thing you just did when you put the caps into
tens".

The printed form of the question matters too: once the idea is in their hands
and the symbol is on the board, put the book's own exercise wording in front of
the class and answer one together (`assessment-alignment`) - in that order,
never before.
