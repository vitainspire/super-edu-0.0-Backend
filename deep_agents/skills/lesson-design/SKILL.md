# Lesson design

One prep material = **one class period**, for the teacher, not the student.

## The six sections

    1. REFRESHER   picks up where the last period's Explore left off
    2. REAL LIFE   the idea as something the class already recognises
    3. CONCEPT     what the textbook actually says, cited by page
    4. CHALLENGE   a hands-on or movement task the class does
    5. LEVEL SET   the misconception, put where believing it shows
    6. EXPLORE     what this opens up - and the hook the next Refresher picks up

The order is not decorative. Real Life before Concept is
concrete-before-symbolic at section scale: the class handles or physically
enacts the thing (standing over a chair to see it from above, walking round it
to see it from the side) in Real Life, and only meets the printed page - the
textbook's own top-view or side-view drawing - once that's done, in Concept.
A plan that opens the period by pointing at the book's picture has run these
two sections in the wrong order even if both are present. Level Set after
Challenge is deliberate: you cannot surface a wrong belief before the child has
had occasion to use it.

## What each section owes the five goals

The goals in `learning-goals` are not extra sections. They are duties **inside**
these six, and this is where they land:

    REFRESHER   collects the take-home question from last period, from one or
                two volunteers, and works fine for a child who did not do it
                (`self-study`). Opens on a question, never a summary.
    REAL LIFE   the tier-1 real thing - in the room, on the body - plus the one
                question the concept decides, i.e. the application
                (`real-world-connection`). Highest participation minutes in the
                period; use them.
    CONCEPT     the page, read against an object still in the children's hands,
                broken by 20-second whole-class checks (`engagement`). One of
                the chapter's printed questions is met here, in the book's own
                wording (`assessment-alignment`).
    CHALLENGE   the floor is reached here by everybody, step-scored; and the
                one-sentence stretch prompt is spoken here, around minute 3-4,
                for whoever has finished (`differentiation`, `stretch-design`).
    LEVEL SET   the misconception, checked with a private-response device so
                the below-grade child answers honestly - then the single
                revisable notebook line (`assessment-alignment`).
    EXPLORE     the take-home question, said aloud and written in one line -
                which is also the seam hook (`self-study`).

A section can carry a duty badly by carrying it abstractly. "Relate to real
life" is not Real Life; "extension for advanced learners" is not a stretch
prompt. Write the sentence the teacher says.

## The seam

**Refresher(N) picks up Explore(N-1).** This is the single mechanism that turns
forty separate sheets into a chapter.

Write both ends in the same words. They are checked for word overlap downstream,
and they were authored to match, so a mismatch is a defect and not a phrasing
difference. If topic 7's Explore ends on "what happens if you fold it twice",
topic 8's Refresher opens on folding it twice - not on "revision of paper
folding".

The first topic of a strand has no previous Explore to pick up. Its Refresher
draws on the chapter prerequisites instead - and where the class may not have a
prerequisite at all, that Refresher is where the 60-second repair happens
(`curriculum-reasoning`).

**The seam must not become load-bearing on work done at home.** Explore(N)
invites a child to go and look at something; Refresher(N+1) collects a couple
of answers and then proceeds using something present in the room. A plan that
requires every child to have brought an object, or done the task, has converted
an invitation into a requirement and locked out the children with the least
time and space at home.

## Time allocation

Thirty minutes on paper. About **22 of real teaching** once settling is counted.
Divide against the trajectory, not evenly:

- The section carrying the **conceptual jump** gets the most minutes. That is
  where children stop following.
- Refresher is short. Two minutes. It is a hook, not a revision lesson.
- Challenge needs its staging time counted, not just its running time. Forty
  children forming rows costs minutes before anything happens.
- **The closure costs 60-90 seconds** at the end of Level Set - the one
  notebook line - and it is not optional, because it is the only thing the
  child can revise from.
- **The take-home question costs about 30 seconds** of speaking at the end of
  Explore, and **shares the closure's notebook moment** - both lines go on the
  board together and the class opens notebooks once. Two separate copying
  moments in one period is a minute nobody has; see `assessment-alignment`.

Everything else the five goals ask for is a change of *how* existing minutes are
spent, not an addition: the real-world thing was already Real Life, the printed
question sits inside Concept's five minutes, the participation devices replace
teacher talk rather than adding to it, and the stretch prompt is one sentence
said to one corner of the room while everybody else keeps working. Budget about
two new minutes for the whole set. If your draft needs more, re-cost it.

A worked division for a 22-minute period, as a starting point to move against
the trajectory - not a template:

    Refresher    2      Challenge    7   (incl. staging + stretch prompt)
    Real Life    4      Level Set    3   (incl. ~90 s closure)
    Concept      5      Explore      1   (incl. ~30 s take-home)

**A rotation-staged Challenge (see `activity-design`) must fit inside
Challenge's share of the 22 minutes, not run to its own schedule.** Rotation
solves the problem of thirty children needing one set of materials; it does not
create extra minutes. Two or three stations at a flat "10 minutes each" is
20-30 minutes by itself - more than a single period usually has left once the
other five sections are costed. Either the station count and dwell time are
sized down to fit Challenge's actual share, or the rotation is designed
deliberately to span this period's Challenge and the next one's, not silently
assumed to fit in one. A plan that imports the rotation model's default timing
unchanged has not done this arithmetic.

A plan that allocates five minutes to each of six sections has not thought about
the concept.

## The vocabulary ledger

Track every term the chapter introduces and **where it is first used**. Two rules
follow:

1. **A term is explained in the same breath it first appears.** Not later, not in
   a glossary, not "as we learned". Where the term is bridged from a home
   language (see `language-support`), the bilingual anchor happens here, in
   this breath - not as a separate pre-teaching step.
2. **A term is not re-explained after that.** A sheet that redefines "numerator"
   in four consecutive periods is a sheet the teacher stops reading. The same
   rule retires a bilingual anchor: once a term has had its held-together
   moment, later sections use the medium-of-instruction word only, matching
   `language-support`'s "move to the medium" step exactly.

Where a term appears before the period that introduces it, that is a chain
defect - flag it rather than papering over it with a second definition.

**The term that reaches the notebook is the term the exam will use.** The
child's own words are the route in; the closure line carries the printed word,
or revision teaches a word nobody will ask about.

## The exercise ledger

The same bookkeeping, for the chapter's **printed questions**: which page and
question numbers each period puts in front of the class, and therefore which
ones no period has covered by the end of the chapter. A child who never meets a
printed question in class can only meet it at home, and home study is not
available here. See `assessment-alignment`; report uncovered items rather than
leaving them silent.

## Cognitive load

Count the new things in one period: new terms, new notation, new procedure, new
context. **More than about three and the period will not land**, whatever the
minutes say. "Top view", "side view", and "triangle" in a single period is
already at that ceiling on terms alone, before counting the new procedure
(tracing, folding) the period also introduces - see `language-support` for why
unfamiliar-in-any-language terms count double against this cap.

The closure line, the take-home question and the stretch prompt do **not** count
against this cap - none of them introduces a new thing to learn. A stretch
prompt that does introduce one (new vocabulary, a new procedure) is the wrong
prompt; re-read `stretch-design`.

If the topic genuinely carries more, that is information about the sequencing,
not a reason to speed up. Say so.

## What lesson design does not decide

- **The order of topics.** Settled deterministically from the book's own
  headings and page breaks. Not negotiable, not yours.
- **What must be learned.** Settled by curriculum reasoning upstream.
- **The words on the final sheet.** Written by generation, downstream, from your
  plan.
