# Literacy

**A language is learned by USING it** - speaking, listening, acting, and playing
with words and sounds - not by copying rules off the board. A grammar rule
written on the blackboard and copied into forty notebooks teaches copying.

## What a literacy trajectory looks like

    hear -> say -> play with the sound -> see it written -> read it back ->
    use it in their own sentence

The conceptual jump is usually at **see it written**: the moment a sound the
child already owns gets attached to a mark. Everything before it is oral and
everything after is practice.

For comprehension rather than decoding:

    picture the scene -> predict -> read -> check the prediction -> say what
    changed their mind

The **floor** sits in the oral half: a child who cannot yet read the word can
still say it, act it, and find something in the room that starts with it. That
is the same road, entered earlier (`differentiation`).

## Oral first, and for longer than feels comfortable

The commonest failure in a primary language period is moving to the written form
in the first five minutes. The child who has said the word twenty times and
acted it out once will read it. The child who met it on the board will copy it.

## Build the bridge from the local language

Especially for English. A new word is never left unexplained, and the bridge
into it starts in the language the child thinks in. See the `language-support`
skill for the three-step bridge.

Do not treat the home language as a problem to be got past. It is the largest
asset in the room.

## Misconceptions this subject reliably produces

- **A letter has one sound.** Then the child meets a word where it does not, and
  concludes they read it wrong.
- **Reading means saying the words.** Decoding fluently with no comprehension
  reads as success on every classroom measure.
- **Longer word, bigger thing.** "Caterpillar" must name something larger than
  "cow".
- **The written form is the real word** and speech is a degraded version of it.
- **A rule has no exceptions**, because every example on the page obeys it.
- **Capital letters are for important words**, generalised from seeing them on
  names.

## Staging that works at forty children

- **Act it out** - the class physically becomes the scene in the story. Free,
  loud, and the best comprehension check there is.
- **Sound hunt** - children find things in the room starting with a sound.
- **Word building with bodies** - each child is a letter or a syllable, the group
  arranges itself into the word.
- **Story with a hole in it** - stop mid-sentence, the class supplies the next
  part, then compare with what the book says.
- **Two-word rewrite** - the class changes two words in a printed sentence and
  says what changed about the meaning.

**Everybody-at-once devices:** say it together; thumbs on the chest for
does-this-sound-the-same; tell your neighbour what happens next; everybody point
at something in the room that starts with the sound. Nobody watches, and the
child who is behind is not exposed (`engagement`).

## Writing is a thing you do to be read

A writing task with no reader is copying. Give the sentence somewhere to go -
read aloud, passed to the next row, put on the wall.

## Take-home questions that cost nothing

Spoken, remembered, needing no adult and no page (`self-study`):

- find three things at home whose name starts with the same sound as your own
  name
- ask nobody, just listen: what is the loudest sound at your house in the
  evening? Tomorrow we will find a word for it
- tell somebody the story we read today, and see if they ask you a question you
  could not answer
- find the longest word you can see written anywhere between school and home

The third one is the strongest of these: retelling is the comprehension check,
and the child does it without a book.

## Stretch prompts, same materials

- "change two words in that sentence so it means the opposite"
- "find a word where that letter does *not* make the sound we said"
- "tell the same story from the crow's side"
- "make a sentence where every word starts with the same sound"
- "what would you have to change to make this story happen at night?"

All open, all answered out loud to a neighbour, none marked
(`stretch-design`).

## The exam asks in the book's words

Reading and grammar papers reuse the textbook's own question forms - "match the
following", "fill in the blank", "answer in one sentence". Once a word or idea
has been used orally, put the printed question in front of the class and answer
one together, in class (`assessment-alignment`). The child who has only ever
met the idea in speech loses the marks.
