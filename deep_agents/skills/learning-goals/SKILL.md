# Learning goals

`core-pedagogy` says what the room is. This file says **what a period has to
achieve in it**. The chain, the trajectory, the activity, the six sections and
the adaptation patch are all judged against these five outcomes. A stage that
satisfies its own local rules and misses these has produced a correct artifact
that teaches nobody.

## The five

**G1 - the below-grade child gets the concept and what it is for.**
Not a simplified concept. The same concept, reached by a route that starts at
something the child can already do, in language they already think in, and
arriving at a use they can recognise. "And what it is for" is half the goal:
a child who can name a top view and cannot say when anybody would want one has
learned a word.
*Not this:* short sentences and easy words wrapped around a demand the child
cannot perform (see demand vs content in `core-pedagogy`).
*Test:* name the one thing the slowest child in the room leaves able to do, and
the question they can now answer that they could not answer this morning. If
you cannot, the period has no floor. See `differentiation`.

**G2 - the child engages actively, through the world in front of them.**
Two halves: *active* (the child is doing, saying or deciding something, not
watching) and *real* (the idea is attached to something in this room, on this
body, in this playground - not to an imagined stadium or an unverified
village). Recognition is the mechanism; a real-world example the child does
not already know is decoration.
*Not this:* the teacher naming a relatable example while forty children
listen. That is a mention, not engagement.
*Test:* at every point in the period, what is the whole class doing? And is
there one moment where the concept **decides** something about a thing in the
room? See `engagement` and `real-world-connection`.

**G3 - the child reaches a passing grade on class time alone.**
The textbook is what the child is examined on, and home study is not available
to assume: no second book, no printables, no guaranteed quiet space, no
guaranteed literate adult. So everything a pass requires has to happen inside
the 22 minutes - including meeting the chapter's own printed questions in the
form the exam will use them, and leaving the child's notebook holding
something they can revise from.
*Not this:* "the class should practise the exercises at home."
*Test:* which printed question of this chapter did this period put in front of
the class, and what one revisable line is now in the notebook? See
`assessment-alignment`.

**G4 - the child can keep exploring after the bell, alone.**
One question the world answers, needing nothing bought, printed, charged or
supervised, and self-checkable by the child. This is the only mechanism in the
pipeline that reaches the other 23 hours.
*Not this:* homework. Nothing about the next period may depend on it having
been done, and no child is ever penalised for not doing it.
*Test:* could a child with no adult, no money and no light after 7pm do this
and know whether they got it? See `self-study`.

**G5 - the capable child is genuinely stretched.**
The child who finishes in half the time is otherwise the pipeline's quiet
casualty: they are the one being taught least. Stretch means demand raised on
the same content with the same materials - invent, break, justify, generalise -
not more items or an extra sheet.
*Not this:* the same task again with bigger numbers, or a printed extension.
*Test:* name the open prompt a fast finisher gets at minute 18, and check it
costs nothing to run. See `differentiation` and `stretch-design`.

## They are simultaneous, not a menu

G1 and G5 point in opposite directions and the temptation is to split the class
in two. Do not. **One task, three depths** is the pattern that survives one
teacher: the same object, the same instruction, and a step-scored path with an
open end. A period with a separate track for the strugglers is a period the one
teacher cannot run, and it tells thirty children which track they are on.

The resolution for every pair that seems to conflict:

    G1 vs G5      same task, step-scored at the bottom, open at the top
    G1 vs G3      floor first, exam wording second - never the reverse
    G2 vs the
    provenance
    rules         use the room and the body (needs no evidence), not the
                  village (needs verified evidence)
    G4 vs equity  the take-home is invited and self-checked, never assumed
    G3 vs
    understanding concrete first, then the question in the book's own words.
                  Drilling the wording of an idea the child has not handled
                  produces a pass this year and nothing next year

## The minute arithmetic

Five goals do not buy five extra stages. The 22 minutes are already spent, and
a plan that adds one section per goal is a plan that will not run. Four of the
five are **re-uses of sections you already have**, and the whole set costs
about two minutes of new time:

    G1  0 min   route and wording of what is already there
    G2  0 min   Real Life already exists; engagement devices replace teacher
                talk rather than adding to it
    G3  ~90 s   the closure line at the end of Level Set
    G4  ~30 s   one spoken question at the end of Explore
    G5  0 min   a prompt for whoever finishes early; nobody else stops

If your plan needs more than that, you have added activities instead of
redesigning the ones you had. Cut back to the sections in `lesson-design` and
put the goals *inside* them.

## Where each goal is owned

Nobody owns a goal alone, but each has a home:

    G1  differentiation, language-support, core-pedagogy
    G2  real-world-connection, engagement, activity-design
    G3  assessment-alignment, lesson-design, grounding
    G4  self-study, lesson-design (the Explore seam)
    G5  stretch-design, differentiation, activity-design

## The gate: critique your own draft before you submit it

Run these on your own output. They are yes/no on purpose - a goal with no
observable test is decoration, and every one of these failures reads as a good
artifact on the page:

1. **Floor named?** One sentence, child's voice, physically demonstrable, for
   the child furthest behind. Absent = G1 fails silently.
2. **Application present?** One question outside the textbook that the concept
   answers. Absent = a definition was taught.
3. **Participation counted?** No stretch longer than about 90 seconds in which
   only the teacher acts. Violated = G2 fails while the plan looks lively.
4. **Real thing, present tense?** The example is in the room, on the body, or
   in the resource profile - not imagined and not inferred from where the
   school is.
5. **Exam contact made?** A printed question of this chapter met in class, in
   its own wording.
6. **Notebook line left?** One thing a child can revise from tonight without
   help.
7. **Take-home free and self-checkable?** No cost, no adult, no print, and the
   child can tell whether they got it. And nothing downstream depends on it.
8. **Ceiling open?** A stretch prompt using the same materials, open-ended,
   with no marking cost.
9. **Standard intact?** Nothing above shrank the target. Floors, scaffolds and
   step-scoring reach the same target; a smaller target is forbidden however
   it is dressed (`contextual-reinforcement`).
10. **Minutes still add up?** Re-cost the period. Goals do not create time.

Where a check genuinely cannot be met for this topic, **say which one and why**
rather than writing something thin that passes it on paper. A declared gap is
actionable downstream; a decorative closer is not.

**The anti-boilerplate test.** Every one of these can be passed with a sentence
that would fit any topic in any chapter. If your stretch prompt, application or
take-home question would work verbatim for a different topic, it is boilerplate
and it has passed the check without meeting the goal. Each must name **this
period's object, number or word**:

    boilerplate  "encourage advanced learners to explore further"
    boilerplate  "relate the concept to the students' daily lives"
    boilerplate  "ask students to practise at home"
    real         "find something in this room where the top and the side look
                 the same"

## When the period runs out of minutes

It will. Something is dropped in the room whatever the sheet says, so decide
the order here rather than leaving the teacher to guess:

    drop first    the stretch prompt (one sentence, one child, next period
                  meets the same idea)
    then          the take-home question
    then          the second half of the application
    never         the floor, and never the closure line

The floor and the closure line are the two things a child cannot get anywhere
else: the floor is the only guarantee the below-grade child was taught, and the
notebook line is the only page they own. Everything above them is valuable and
recoverable next period.

## What none of these licenses

- Inventing local facts to make G2 land (`contextual-reinforcement`).
- Raising the required demand above the grade band to make G5 land. G5 raises
  demand for whoever accepts an invitation, never for the class or the
  assessment (`stretch-design`).
- Naming a material the room does not have to make G4 or G5 land.
- Reducing what a child ends up able to do to make G1 or G3 land. This is the
  one that never trades.
