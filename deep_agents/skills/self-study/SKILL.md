# Self study

The pipeline reaches 30 minutes a day. This is the only mechanism that touches
the other 23 hours, and it is worth designing properly rather than ending the
sheet with "practise at home".

## The five conditions

A take-home question is legitimate only if **every** child in the room can do
it. Fail one condition and the question excludes precisely the children it was
supposed to help:

1. **No cost.** Nothing bought, nothing printed, nothing borrowed. Objects a
   home already has - a matchbox, a bucket, a plate, a door, a shadow, a
   vegetable being cut for cooking, the child's own steps across a room.
2. **No adult.** Not "ask your parents". A parent may be absent, working, or
   unable to read the medium of instruction, and a question that needs them
   turns into a question about whose family can help.
3. **No print, no screen.** The child has their notebook and the world.
4. **Answerable by looking or doing**, tonight, in a few minutes. Not a
   project.
5. **Self-checkable.** The child can tell whether they got it - either because
   the world answers (the box refolds or it does not; the two counts agree or
   they do not) or because tomorrow one minute of class compares answers.

Condition 5 is the one usually missing, and without it the child cannot learn
from doing it - they can only wait to be told.

## The form

**One question, not a list.** A single question a child can hold in their head
walking home; five instructions require a written sheet the child does not
have.

    weak    revise the difference between top view and side view
    strong  find one thing at home that looks like a circle from the top.
            Tomorrow we will see if anyone found the same thing.

    weak    practise counting in tens
    strong  count how many steps it takes you to cross your room. Ask a
            younger child to do it too. Who took more steps? Why, when the
            room did not change?

    weak    complete exercise 4 at home
    strong  take any empty box at home and open it out flat with your hands.
            Can you fold it back the same way? Which flap gave you trouble?

Notice what the strong versions have: a **thing to do with the hands**, an
answer the child can see, and a reason to come back and say something.

## It is the Explore hook, not an extra section

`lesson-design` requires Explore(N) and Refresher(N+1) to be two ends of one
seam, written in the same words. The self-study question **is** that hook:

    Explore(N)      "Take any empty box at home and open it out flat. Which
                    flap gave you trouble?"
    Refresher(N+1)  "Who opened out a box? Which flap gave you trouble?"

This costs about 30 seconds of the period and strengthens the seam rather than
competing with it: the next Refresher opens on a question children already have
an opinion about, which is worth far more than a summary of yesterday.

## And it can never be load-bearing

**Refresher(N+1) must work perfectly for a child who did not do it.** A child
was pulled into work, had no light, had no box, was ill. Design the opening so
that:

- The question is asked of the room, and **one or two answers are enough** to
  restart the idea for everybody.
- Anything the class needs in order to proceed is available in the room. If the
  box matters, the teacher's own matchbox is on the desk.
- Nobody is asked who did not do it, nobody is counted, nobody is penalised.

Where the plan says "the class will have brought X", the plan has turned an
invitation into a requirement. Rewrite it.

## Curiosity, not compliance

The purpose is that the idea keeps working in the child's head after the bell,
so the question is one they might actually want to answer. Prefer:

    a surprise      "find something that looks completely different from the
                    top than from the side"
    a comparison    "who in your house has the biggest hand? Trace it with
                    your finger - is it the same shape as yours?"
    a prediction    "guess how many caps fill a cup, then check"
    a hunt          "find three round things and one that is nearly round"

Avoid anything that reads as unpaid extra work, and avoid anything a child
could get wrong in a way nobody will notice - that is where misconceptions
harden.

## Say it, and write it once

The teacher says it aloud in the last minute, and it goes into the notebook as
the shortest possible line - because a spoken question is forgotten by the gate
and a five-line instruction is not copied.

**It rides on the closure moment, it does not add a second one.** Forty
children open their notebooks once per period (`assessment-alignment`): the
closure line, then this question beneath it in a handful of words - "open a box
flat: which flap?" - both on the board together while the class copies once.
A take-home that needs its own notebook moment costs a minute the period does
not have and will be the first thing the teacher cuts.

## What not to do

- No adult, no purchase, no phone, no printout. Restated because it is the
  failure that keeps coming back in a new costume ("just watch one video
  about...").
- Nothing that asks the child to reveal what their home has or does. "Find a
  round thing" is safe; "show us your family's cooking pots" is not
  (`real-world-connection`, and check 7 of the equity gate).
- Nothing unsafe or unsupervised: no climbing, no water, no fire, no cutting,
  no going anywhere to look at something.
- Not graded, not collected, not marked.
