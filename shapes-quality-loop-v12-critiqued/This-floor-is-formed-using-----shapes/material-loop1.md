# Identify shapes that fit without gaps
_This floor is formed using ___ shapes._

> This period focuses on the concept of tiling and gaps, building on students' understanding of shape properties from the previous lesson. It uses familiar objects like matchboxes and bangles to demonstrate how some shapes fit together perfectly while others leave gaps.

## Refresher — 4. Let us observe the shapes in a rangoli.
1. **Door and table have straight sides** — Ask students what they noticed about the sides and corners of a door or table at home. Listen for descriptions of straight sides and clear corners.
2. **Round objects have no corners** — Ask students what they found about the round objects at home. Confirm that they have no straight sides or corners, unlike doors and tables.
3. **Shapes have specific properties** — Remind students that shapes like squares, triangles, and circles are defined by their specific properties, like having straight sides and corners, or being round.

## Concept
1. **Identify shapes in classroom floor patterns** — Point to a tiled section of the classroom floor. Ask students to identify the shapes they see. Give them 10 seconds to look and think.
2. **Matchboxes fit without leaving gaps** — Hold up two matchboxes and place them flush against each other on the blackboard. Draw the outline of the two matchboxes, showing no gaps. Explain that their straight edges allow them to touch perfectly.
3. **Bangles leave gaps when placed together** — Hold up two bangles and place them next to each other on the blackboard. Draw the outline of the two bangles, showing the space between them. Explain that their curved edges mean they cannot touch perfectly everywhere, leaving gaps.

## Real Life
1. **Floor tiles fit with no gaps** — Ask students to look at the classroom floor. Point out how the tiles fit together without any spaces. Explain that this is why the floor is smooth and even. Allow students to observe quietly.
2. **Brick walls show no gaps** — Describe a brick wall. Explain that bricks, like matchboxes, have straight edges and fit together tightly to make a strong wall without gaps. This is why houses are sturdy.
3. **Arranging bangles leaves spaces** — Imagine arranging many bangles on a table. Explain that no matter how you arrange them, there will always be small spaces between them because of their curved shape. This is different from how bricks or tiles fit.

## Challenge — Identify the shapes used to form different floors
1. **Arrange matchboxes without gaps** — Students work in pairs with matchboxes. They try to arrange them on their slate to cover as much area as possible without leaving any gaps, as shown in the book (Page None). Give them a few minutes to experiment.
2. **Arrange bangles, observe gaps** — Pairs then take bangles or coins and try to arrange them on their slate. They should observe and discuss how bangles leave gaps, unlike the matchboxes (Page None). Encourage them to try different arrangements.
3. **Identify shapes in floor patterns** — Direct students to the floor patterns in the book (Page None). In pairs, they identify the shapes used to form the floor and discuss why these shapes fit together without gaps. Confirm the shapes and the reason.

## Level Set
1. **Floor gaps: wrong shapes or arrangement?** — Ask students if a floor has gaps because the shapes are wrong, or because they are arranged in a way that creates gaps. Have them discuss with a partner. Listen for students who might blame the shape itself.
2. **Tick-list for fitting shapes** — Have partners list two shapes that fit without gaps and two that leave gaps. They should check each other's lists. Watch for correct identification of tiling vs. non-tiling shapes.
3. **Find tiling patterns on the way home** — On the way home, or at home, look for patterns where shapes fit together without any gaps. This could be floor tiles, brick walls, or even a woven mat. Notice what shapes are used.

## Explore
*Board sketch: matchboxes arranged without gaps, bangles with gaps*

1. **Look at a wall, notice brick shapes** — Look closely at a brick wall in your village. Notice the shape of the bricks and how they fit together without leaving spaces.
2. **Find patterns on a footpath** — When walking on a footpath, look for any patterns made by stones or tiles. See if those shapes fit together perfectly or if there are gaps.
3. **Observe a bicycle wheel** — Look at a bicycle wheel. Notice its round shape. Imagine trying to cover a floor with many bicycle wheels. Think about whether they would leave gaps.

## Watch For
- **Refresher:** Students recall shape properties — Ensure students can clearly differentiate between shapes based on their properties, which is crucial for understanding why some shapes tile and others don't.
- **Concept:** Students grasp tiling vs. gaps — Observe if students understand the core idea that straight edges allow shapes to fit without gaps, while curved edges create them. Look for confusion between shape and arrangement.
- **Explore:** Students observe tiling in environment — Ensure students understand they are looking for patterns where shapes fit together or leave gaps, specifically in their surroundings.
- **Challenge:** Pairs demonstrate tiling and gaps — Check if pairs successfully arrange matchboxes without gaps and identify gaps when arranging bangles. Observe their discussions about the floor patterns in the book.
- **Level Set:** Students apply tiling understanding — Listen for students who attribute gaps to 'wrong shapes' rather than the inherent properties of the shape. Guide them to understand the geometric reason for gaps.

## Materials
- matchbox
- bangle
- slate
- paper

## Timings
Refresher: 5 min, Concept: 4 min, Reallife: 5 min, Challenge: 6 min, Level Set: 4 min, Explore: 6 min (Total: 30 min)
